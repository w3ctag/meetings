## Notes

Brett introduces his angle on commons study:

* Brett is interdisciplinary. All the participants come at this differently. You have lots of nested commons, at every layer. Even at the infrastructural level, layers.
* What does "The Web" mean?
    * Jeffrey: The linking structure of URLs that's mostly in HTTP and HTML. Mostly used by browsers, but not exclusively.
* Commons always refer to the set of institutions that a community use to share resources. Distinguish the resources from the governance structure.
* Idea that the W3C generates sharable resources (infrastructural knowledge commons). (Book on Infrastructure resources) They overlap. Standards that are widely used to enable interoperability, are infrastructural knowledge commons. Shareable knowledge that lots of people rely on to build other things. Commons too: community governed, subject to processes. Would be interesting to do a GKC study on the W3C as an example of a knowledge commons.
* The Web: break it down and say what we're talking about.  May be the case that there are nested knowledge commons built on the standards. E.g. Wikipedia is a knowledge commons that depends on the infrastructure of the Web.
* Even firms run proprietary sites where they manage knowledge commons within their sites. There are differences between Wikipedia and World of Warcraft (which also contains knowledge commons), but they're both KCs.
* Brett's been writing about friction in design, CAPTCHAs, digital contracts. Better mechanisms for helping people understand what they're doing. Attention economy. People having time to deliberate about what they're doing instead of just following scripts.
* Robots.txt issue is a dilemma the W3C is facing. Whatever kind of site you are, everyone's running into the shared dilemma about authenticating. Need to know who the visitors are. CAPTCHA has historically worked reasonably well; people have historically obeyed the robots.txt suggestion. There's a need to evolve the consenting mechanism. More than on-off.
    * Jeffrey elaborates on the problems robots.text might solve.
* Crawlers aren't members of a community who obey rules.
* e.g. There's a collision in a park. So community passes a rule "no vehicles in the park". Law schools use that to ask "what is a vehicle". Brett uses it as an example of how to solve the community problem, with lots of options.
* Why does a social norm work? Only works in a close-knit community, where people care. So then think about rules about internet content. How do we then go through the possible approaches. robots.txt is a workable social-norm kind of problem. but it runs into limits. If you have a huge park or small town, might not care about occasional violations. But when you get more traffic, it becomes a problem. robots.txt: how to devise a hybrid kind of solution?
* Need a solution to non-members of the community, across lots of the web's knowledge commonses.
* From a commons perspective, it's a dilemma about establishing a boundary. Or a "governance seam" around the resource. Bring in "friction in design" too.
* One of the first questions is "what are the community, resources, and objectives?"
* Commons research can be useful to break things down, and see how things are nested.
* Maybe think about how different communities within the Web need different things.
* Robots.txt might be one of many dilemmas.
