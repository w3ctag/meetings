* Basic structure of the Web as a commons:
    * Websites rely on a population of users who look for information on the Web and are willing to look at ads or otherwise compensate the sites.
        * Sites can fail to invest in the commons by wasting users' time, for example by being content farms or putting up too many ads and consent screens.
    * Users rely on a population of sites with useful content, but because visiting sites isn't (very) rivalrous, they're basically a public good. Still, if enough users free-ride, by installing ad-blockers or using AI, Search, or Browsers that scrape information off sites without supporting those sites, that can undercut the provision of the public good.
    * This is a different structure from Open Source, where nearly all appropriators have the skills to also provide the resource they're appropriating.
    * There are many smaller commons and public goods tied up in this, including standards, advertising, shared search resources, etc. We could get lost charting them all, but some are probably important.
    * Historically, Google has (probably) been the "dominant actor in a privileged group" and has invested a lot in supporting the commons. How has that changed over time, has Google lost track of that responsibility in favor of a higher discount rate, and how does antitrust change things?

    * Ostrom’s framework helps us understand why the early promise of the web wasn't actually realized. Her literature was about how people share things. The institutional circumstances under which sharing doesn’t fail.
    * Nobel prize for disproving the economic theory that people can't share things.
    * She fostered a big network of academics to identify principles. (ie the “Bloomington School”) The principles aren’t a formula, but are a set of patterns that tend to characterize successful sharing systems; failure of sharing systems tends to reflect the lack or breakdown of at least one of the principles. 
* Tristan: Is there a cheat sheet?
    * Greg: Various popularizers. See [David Bowlier, Silke Helfrich. ](https://www.bollier.org/strengthen-commons-now)
        * I've also translated this work into the language of open source: [https://sustainoss.pubpub.org/pub/jqngsp5u](https://sustainoss.pubpub.org/pub/jqngsp5u)
    * Ostrom developed these principles by studying nature-based resources. Toward the end of her career, she applied it to Knowledge Commons. See [the Governing Knowledge Commons](https://knowledge-commons.net/research-framework/) sub-domain.
        1. Clear boundaries. 
            * This is hard for fans of open access. Isn’t the point of open that anyone can do anything?
            * GB finds boundaries in scope, and also in values. Values guide institutional decision-making within a set of normative boundaries. [See this paper draft, pending publication](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1Ijxw-TUZRn1Jb5xqUBxKKIflkxsGiDE6LX-M6awRYps/edit)
        2. Clear and fair agreements about terms for use.
        3. Rules for making and changing agreements through accountable processes that impacted parties can participate in – i.e. collective choice agreements
        4. Monitoring
        5. Graduated sanctions. (e.g. slap on the wrist up to banishment) 
            * This might also entail incentives for good behavior alongside punishment for bad behavior.
        6. Conflict resolution mechanisms
        7. State must permit this self-governance, as a community.
        8. Nested, subsidiary, polycentric levels of all of the above. (Local, regional, etc)
    * This reflects how the US governments are designed. However, we’ve lost the knack for this. On some level these are instinctive ideas, but we're not educated in this in school, and US culture socialized people to believe it’s not possible to cooperate. 
    * Applying these principles to knowledge resources: they sometimes apply, and sometimes have to be adjusted for non-rivalrous or non-exhaustible knowledge resources.
        * GB: Verification (is information valid, true?) may be as important or more important than punishment for designing sustainable knowledge commons
    * Early internet evangelists got excited about non-rivalry of data resources, and thought they didn't need to do politics for these commons to work. But that wasn't true. You still have dilemmas in data resource systems – collective action problems that we have to figure out how to address.
* Dan: How does this get applied to an adjacent ecosystem, which is a functioning commons? For example, Wikipedia? How do the rules apply? Anyone can come in and use it?
